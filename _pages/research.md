Eicosanoids role in prostate and ovarian cancer during <em>Phoenix rising</em>
======
<p style="text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 1em;">
Eicosanoids, particularly prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), play a key role in the <em>Phoenix Rising</em> response by mediating compensatory proliferation following therapy-induced apoptosis. In prostate cancer cells, caspase-3 activation stimulates the cPLA2–COX-2–PGE2 pathway, promoting the repopulation of surviving cells following chemotherapy. PGE2 signalling supports tumour cell proliferation and, in cancer cells, is associated with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), epigenetic reprogramming and acquired chemoresistance. In ovarian cancer, the involvement of PGE2 and other eicosanoids in tumour progression and therapy resistance has been investigated, although their specific contribution to Phoenix Rising remains less clearly established. Thus, eicosanoid signalling represents a potential link between therapy-induced cell death, tumour repopulation and cancer relapse. </p>

<p style="text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 1em;">
The goal of the project was to determine the paracrine signalling of prostaglandins, with a focus on PGE2, in inducing epigenetic reprogramming and EMT following Etoposide and Carbonplatin treatments. We employed both a specific <em>in vitro</em> model (CRAC) and an <em> in vivo Drosophila</em> genetic model performing cellular, molecular and multi-omics experiments. </p>

<strong>Main questions</strong> <br>
<em>What is the role of PGE2 in inducing EMT? Which transcription factors are involved in epigenetic reprogramming during repopulation? What interactions between PGE2 and other eicosanoid are responsible of post-therapy signalling and repopulation?</em>

Cell adaptive responses to micro- and nanoplastics and their role in disease progression
======
<p style="text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 1em;">
Micro- and nanoplastics are the most widespread pollutants in the world, having evolved from being emerging pollutants to representing a global emergency. They have been found in all ecosystems and are spreading throughout the food chain (from plants to top predators). In humans, they have been detected in all tissues. They have been shown to cause inflammation, oxidative stress, genotoxicity, genomic instability and DNA damage. Furthermore, they have been linked to Alzheimer’s disease and atherosclerotic plaques. </p>

<p style="text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 1em;">
The aim of the project was to analyse the molecular pathways that respond to the presence of micro- and nanoplastics both <em>in vitro</em> and <em>in vivo</em>, with a particular focus on intestinal and renal tissues. Furthermore, we investigated their role in the onset and progression of cancer, as well as in the severity of various genetic diseases. </p>

<strong>Main questions</strong> <br>
<em>What are the biological effects of micro- and nanoplastics in intestinal and renal tissues? How do cells respond/adapt to their presence? Do they influence genetic disease severity and cancer initiation and progression?</em>

Bicaudal-C functions and conservation
======
<p style="text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 1em;">
Bicaudal-C (BICC1) is an evolutionarily conserved RNA-binding protein that regulates gene expression at the post-transcriptional level by controlling mRNA stability, translation and cell localization. It was first identified in <em>Drosophila melanogaster</em> through genetic screenings of mutants displaying a characteristic bicaudal phenotype, with posterior structures duplicated at the anterior pole. Bicaudal-C plays important roles in embryonic development, oogenesis and cell differentiation by regulating the expression of specific genes. In vertebrates, it is involved in the development and function of several organs, particularly the kidney and intestine. Alterations in BICC1 have been associated with human diseases, especially cystic kidney disorders and developmental anomalies. </p>

<p style="text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 1em;">
In this project, we investigated the role of Bicaudal-C genetic modifiers in inducing disease-like phenotypes. To do so, we crossed <em>Drosophila</em> hypomorphyc mutants with the entire DGRP library (<em>Drosophila melanogaster</em> Reference Panel, 205). Then, we dissected malpighian tubules and reproductive tissues and characterized their phenotypes. At the same time, we aligned <em>Drosophila</em> and marine invertebrate and vertebrate BicC sequences (gene and protein) to link structural changes with fucntional conservation. </p>

<strong>Main questions</strong> <br>
<em>Are there genetic modifiers of BicC expression and functions? How does the differential modulation of BicC manifest in a disease-like phenotype?</em>

Analysis of ancient DNA fragmentation
======
<p style="text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 1em;">
Ancient DNA can be defined as genomic material extracted from archaeological finds. It can be either nuclear or mitochondrial and, in both cases, is often fragmented. The integrity of the DNA is a key factor in reconstructing the identity of the organism from which the remains originate. Therefore, studying the degree of DNA fragmentation is necessary to assess the quality of the DNA and to understand how much information can be derived from that sample. For example, the sex chromosomes (X and Y) are often lost, making it impossible to determine biological sex. </p>

<p style="text-align: justify; margin-bottom: 1em;">
In this project, we extracted ancient DNA from various bones found at archaeological sites and compared the degree of fragmentation in nuclear and mitochondrial DNA. Although the latter is considered more reliable (mitochondria are present in large numbers, so the likelihood of finding well-preserved DNA is greater), we found that the nuclear DNA was less fragmented. Furthermore, we have developed a new index to assess this aspect. </p>

<strong>Main questions</strong> <br>
<em>How can we assess DNA fragmentation from archaeological findings? Has mitochondrial DNA to be preferred over nuclears'? Can we employ fragmented DNA as indicator of specific cell stress and type?</em>
